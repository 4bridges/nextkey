NextKey

Hand over a seed phrase without handing over control

NextKey shares secrets by ENS name, releases them by rules, and restores access with proof of humanness. A human is involved. No human is in control.

The problem

Today a seed phrase lives on paper in a drawer, in a password manager somebody else operates, or in a message you regret sending. All three fail the same way: the moment someone else needs it, either nobody can get to it, or the wrong person already has.

The missing piece was never storage. It was deciding who may open something, and when.

How it works

  1. Place a secret

    A seed phrase, a private key, a credential. It is encrypted before it goes anywhere, with a key that never leaves your machine.

  2. Share it with a name

    anna.eth, not 0x7f3a…. NextKey reads the public key Anna publishes in her own ENS records and wraps this one secret's key to it. She never registered with us.

  3. Anna hears about it

    Through whatever channel she declared in her own ENS records — another text record, read the same way.

  4. If she loses everything, she gets back in

    Her guardians confirm, and a liveness check proves a unique, living person is asking. No backup file required.

  5. Some releases happen without you

    An agent proposes a release when a time lock elapses or you go quiet. It cannot decide one. The condition is evaluated inside a confidential enclave, against rules you wrote yourself.

What is different

Access is a protocol, not our database

Permissions live in an ENSv2 registry you own. Revoke them and they are gone whether or not NextKey still exists. Set an expiry and access ends by itself.

We cannot read your secrets, and that is a property of the code

The owner is a recipient like any other — no master key, no owner-only path. The honest cost is stated rather than hidden: lose your key file and the secret is gone, because there is nobody who could recover it for you.

The agent proposes. It cannot decide

Its ENS role permits one record on one name and nothing else — a boundary enforced by the registry, not by our code being careful. Humans confirm; the process, not a person, decides when that is enough.

Under the hood

ENSv2

Our own subname registry under nextkey.eth; each secret a subname with a Permissioned Resolver. Confidentiality comes from cryptography — the record holds ciphertext. Control comes from protocol roles: who may write, revoke or delegate, scoped per record.

Chainlink CRE

The release condition — a guardian quorum and a waiting period — is evaluated inside a Confidential Workflow. Only a verdict crosses back out, carrying the hash of the public request so anyone can check which request it judged.

World ID

Selfie Check as a risk signal during recovery, not as a login. It answers whether a unique, living person is asking. The image never leaves the device.

Questions people actually ask

How do I safely pass on my seed phrase to someone?

Encrypt it to a key only that person holds, and put the ciphertext somewhere you both can reach.

NextKey does this by name: you name a recipient, it reads the public key they publish in their own ENS records, and wraps the secret's key to it. Nothing readable ever passes through a message, a document, or our servers — and the recipient does not have to sign up anywhere first.

What happens to my crypto if I die or lose access?

Nothing, unless you arranged for it in advance — which is the failure this product exists to address.

You can name guardians and a waiting period. If you stop responding, an agent proposes a release; the guardians confirm; the condition is checked in a confidential enclave. You can cancel at any point before it completes, and the waiting period exists so that you can.

Is social recovery actually safe?

On its own, no — it moves the attack from your device to the people around you.

An attacker who can pressure, impersonate or simply out-wait your guardians is through. NextKey therefore requires both a guardian quorum and a proof that a unique, living human is asking, and starts the waiting period when the quorum is reached rather than when the request was filed — otherwise an attacker files early, lets the window run out quietly, and collects approvals for an instant release.

Can I share a password without both of us using the same password manager?

Yes. The recipient needs an ENS name and a published key, not an account with us.

That is the point of addressing people by name rather than by an identifier inside one company's product.

Does NextKey ever see my secrets?

No. Secrets are encrypted before they leave your machine, and we hold no key that could open them.

This is worth stating precisely, because the phrase is often used loosely. The record on chain is public and anyone can read the ciphertext — that is fine, because it is useless without a key. The owner reaches their own secret through exactly the same grant mechanism as any other recipient. There is no administrative path, which also means there is no support ticket that can recover a lost key file.

What is an ENS name and do I need one?

An ENS name is a readable name that points at things on Ethereum — like anna.eth instead of a long hex address.

To receive a secret you need one, plus a published key. To store and share secrets you need one too. NextKey uses ENS for more than readability: the permissions themselves live there, so they outlive us.

Is this ready to use with real funds?

No. Do not put a seed phrase you actually rely on into it.

NextKey is a prototype built during a hackathon. It runs against an ENSv2 beta deployment on a test network, it has not been audited, and parts of it — the guardian layer in particular — are demonstrated rather than finished.
