Hand over a passphrase, then take it back

The whole NextKey loop, running in this tab: encrypt a passphrase, grant it to a recipient, open it as them, watch a stranger fail, then revoke. No wallet, no account, no ether. The last step is optional and puts it on Sepolia under a name you own.

Never type a seed phrase that guards anything — here or anywhere else.

A box on a web page asking for your recovery phrase is the oldest theft in this industry, and NextKey exists because of it. Use the generator below. It makes a real twelve-word phrase that has never held a coin and never will.

The secret

If it protects anything, clear the box now and press the button instead. Steps 1 to 5 never leave this tab — but step 6 writes to a public chain, and a chain does not forget.

2

Who it is for

A recipient is a public key. It can be one made here so you can finish the loop, or a real one read off a name on the hackathon ENS deployment.

3

Encrypt it, and grant it

One AES-256-GCM key for the secret, then that key wrapped to the recipient with X25519 and HKDF-SHA256. Identical to what the command-line tool does — a grant made here opens there.

4

Open it

Once as the recipient, who holds the key. Once as somebody who does not.

You granted it to a name on chain, and their private key is on their machine, not in this browser. So this page cannot open it — and that is the property the whole thing rests on. They would run: nextkey.mjs open <label> <their identity>

5

Take it back

Revoking clears the grant record and leaves the ciphertext alone. Then try opening it again.

6

Optional — put it on chain

Everything above was arithmetic in your browser. This writes the two records to a name you own on the hackathon ENS deployment, signed by your wallet. Register one at the ENS app first if you have none.

What just happened, and what it proves.

The ciphertext and the grant were both public from the moment they existed. Anyone could read them; nobody could use them. What separated the recipient from everyone else was a private key that was never published, never uploaded and never held by us — so there is no server here to subpoena, breach or shut down, and no version of NextKey that could read your secret if it wanted to.

The stranger's failure is the part worth dwelling on. It was not a permission check that a different build could skip. A different key derives a different wrapping key, and AES-GCM refuses. Access control that is arithmetic cannot be configured away.

Revocation is where the chain earns its place. On this page it was a variable set to null. On chain it is a write to a name, and who may perform that write is decided by ENS roles rather than by us — which is the difference between a promise and a rule.
